# Humano (English)

Use this skill to write text a person would actually read: with context, intent and a voice that doesn't sound stamped out of a mold. The goal is not to hide facts or fool a detector. The goal is to cut generic, over-polished, template-shaped writing and replace it with text that is specific, natural and useful.

## Usage rules

- Apply automatically to any English text meant for human readers: post, caption, script, VSL, copy, email, text message, landing page, sales page, bio, article, announcement, newsletter, pitch, speech or editorial.
- Do not apply to code, data, spreadsheets, technical debugging, terminal summaries, API explanations, source extraction or normal technical answers, unless the user asks for a text people will read.
- Write in American English by default. If the user names a market (UK, Australia, Canada, India…), switch to its spelling, vocabulary, date format and punctuation, and keep it from start to finish.
- Use contractions where a person speaking would (you're, it's, don't, we'll). Leave them out only in formal or legal registers.
- Match the tone to the channel. A text message can be short and blunt. A sales page can carry more structure. A bio has to be tight. An article can breathe.
- Prefer concrete detail over abstract praise. Use what the user gave you. Don't invent proof, numbers, dates, testimonials, credentials or sources.
- Vary the rhythm: short and medium sentences, some texture, plain verbs and less artificial symmetry.
- Strip out generic openers, inflated adjectives, false certainty, decorative transitions and chatbot phrases.
- When humanizing a text, keep its meaning, facts, offer and intent, unless the user asks for a different angle.
- Deliver the final text without behind-the-scenes commentary, unless the user asks for a diagnosis, alternatives or an explanation.

## Daily workflow

1. Identify the reader, market, channel, goal and how close the relationship is.
2. Start with the most concrete fact, tension, scene, promise or pain point.
3. Swap out the AI tells listed below.
4. Read it in your head out loud and cut any line that sounds like a template, a pitch deck or an automated support reply.
5. Run the final checklist before delivering.

## Catalog of AI tells

### Content and structure

1. **Generic opener**
   - Why it gives you away: it starts from a safe, universal place instead of the real situation. English AI text leans hard on "In today's fast-paced world…", "In the ever-evolving landscape of…", "In the digital age…" and "Now more than ever…".
   - Bad: "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, a strong online presence is essential."
   - Write instead: "Your customers check your Instagram before they ever send you a message."

2. **Inflated significance**
   - Why it gives you away: it turns ordinary facts into "milestones", "game-changers" or "pivotal moments".
   - Bad: "This launch marks a pivotal milestone for the industry."
   - Write instead: "It's the brand's first launch since 2022."

3. **Forced link to a bigger trend**
   - Why it gives you away: it ties the topic to a larger movement without showing proof.
   - Bad: "The campaign reflects a broader shift in how consumers engage with brands."
   - Write instead: "The campaign started after customers kept asking for smaller orders."

4. **Generic legacy language**
   - Why it gives you away: it talks about lasting impact before showing what changed. Watch for "a testament to", "left an indelible mark", "stands as a beacon".
   - Bad: "The program stands as a testament to the community's resilience."
   - Write instead: "After the program, the school added two more classes."

5. **"Plays a pivotal role"**
   - Why it gives you away: one of the most frequent AI crutches in English ("plays a crucial role", "plays a key role in shaping").
   - Bad: "Communication plays a pivotal role in team success."
   - Write instead: "When the brief shows up half-finished, the team redoes the work on Friday."

6. **Shallow analysis tacked on with "-ing"**
   - Why it gives you away: it adds a trailing clause that interprets instead of informing: "…, highlighting…", "…, underscoring…", "…, showcasing…", "…, reflecting…", "…, ensuring…".
   - Bad: "The company switched platforms, underscoring its commitment to innovation."
   - Write instead: "The company replaced its old system with one it built in-house."

7. **Over-explaining why it matters**
   - Why it gives you away: it explains the importance instead of showing the consequence.
   - Bad: "This highlights the importance of a strategic, holistic approach."
   - Write instead: "Without it, the team loses hours redoing the same task."

8. **Inflated cultural heritage**
   - Why it gives you away: it calls any place or tradition "a rich tapestry", "a vibrant melting pot" or "a hidden gem nestled in…".
   - Bad: "Nestled in the heart of the valley, the town boasts a rich cultural tapestry."
   - Write instead: "The town has a farmers market on Sundays and three street festivals a year."

9. **Generic ecosystem or conservation claims**
   - Why it gives you away: it says a species, place or project is vital without a source or detail.
   - Bad: "The species plays a vital role in maintaining the delicate balance of the ecosystem."
   - Write instead: "The sources provided don't say what role the species plays in its habitat."

10. **Hammered notability**
    - Why it gives you away: it tries to prove relevance by repeating "widely covered", "prominent outlets" or "garnered significant attention".
    - Bad: "The artist has garnered widespread acclaim from prominent media outlets."
    - Write instead: "The Boston Globe ran a profile of her in 2022, and NPR interviewed her in 2024."

11. **Source as decoration**
    - Why it gives you away: it cites a source for a trivial fact, or uses the source as a whiff of authority.
    - Bad: "According to local media, the diner is known for staying open late."
    - Write instead: "The diner is open until 2 a.m. on Fridays and Saturdays."

12. **Online presence as merit**
    - Why it gives you away: it treats posting online as proof of relevance.
    - Bad: "The brand maintains an active presence across social media platforms."
    - Write instead: "The brand uses Instagram to announce restocks."

13. **Vague authority**
    - Why it gives you away: it hides who said something behind "experts", "observers" or "critics".
    - Bad: "Experts say the technique is promising."
    - Write instead: "In a 2023 study, Dr. Sarah Chen called the technique promising."

14. **One source turned into consensus**
    - Why it gives you away: it turns a single opinion into a widely held view.
    - Bad: "The book has been praised by critics."
    - Write instead: "Tom Reyes praised the book in the Chicago Tribune."

15. **Invented debate**
    - Why it gives you away: it claims something "sparks conversations" about big ideas without naming the conversation.
    - Bad: "The film sparks important conversations about identity, memory and belonging."
    - Write instead: "The film follows a woman moving back to the town where she grew up."

16. **Challenges-and-future formula**
    - Why it gives you away: it closes with "despite the challenges, the future looks bright".
    - Bad: "Despite these challenges, the company remains poised to shape the future."
    - Write instead: "The company still has to fix late deliveries and slow customer support."

17. **School-essay conclusion**
    - Why it gives you away: it ends by solemnly restating the thesis and adds nothing. "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "At the end of the day" and "In summary" are the usual flags.
    - Bad: "In conclusion, it's clear this topic is important and deserves attention."
    - Write instead: "Bottom line: without a process, the team runs on improvisation."

18. **Title treated as a thing**
    - Why it gives you away: it defines a list, category or broad topic as if it were a real object.
    - Bad: "List of songs about New York is a curated compilation that…"
    - Write instead: "The list collects songs that mention New York in the title or lyrics."

19. **Template structure**
    - Why it gives you away: it always follows the same mold: context, three benefits, CTA.
    - Bad: "First, understand the landscape. Next, explore the benefits. Finally, take action."
    - Write instead: start from the reader's real tension and move only to what the text needs.

### Vocabulary and sentence patterns

20. **Concentrated AI vocabulary**
    - Why it gives you away: it piles up words like "delve", "tapestry", "testament", "landscape", "realm", "pivotal", "crucial", "robust", "seamless", "vibrant", "meticulous", "intricate", "multifaceted", "navigate", "leverage", "harness", "foster", "elevate", "unlock", "unleash", "empower", "embark", "underscore", "showcase", "boasts".
    - Bad: "Let's delve into how this robust solution empowers leaders to navigate the evolving landscape."
    - Write instead: "It's a tool that lets managers see delays before the meeting."

21. **Corporate perfume**
    - Why it gives you away: it sounds polished but says little.
    - Bad: "Unlock your full potential with a seamless, all-in-one solution."
    - Write instead: "See which ads sell and shut off the ones that just burn budget."

22. **Dodging the simple verb**
    - Why it gives you away: it swaps "is", "has", "uses" or "does" for "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "represents".
    - Bad: "The platform serves as a comprehensive learning ecosystem."
    - Write instead: "The platform has lessons, exercises and feedback in one place."

23. **Marketing verb for a neutral fact**
    - Why it gives you away: "offers", "features", "boasts" and "provides" inflate basic information.
    - Bad: "The apartment boasts three bedrooms and features a balcony."
    - Write instead: "The apartment has three bedrooms and a balcony."

24. **Faux-casual hook**
    - Why it gives you away: it fakes intimacy with the same handful of lines: "Here's the thing.", "Let that sink in.", "Spoiler alert:", "And honestly?", "The kicker?", "Let's dive in."
    - Bad: "Here's the thing: most founders get this wrong. Let that sink in."
    - Write instead: "Most founders price by gut feeling and only notice when cash runs out."

25. **"Not only X, but also Y"**
    - Why it gives you away: it builds drama where one plain sentence would do.
    - Bad: "The course not only teaches sales but also transforms your mindset."
    - Write instead: "The course teaches sales with weekly exercises."

26. **"It's not just X, it's Y"**
    - Why it gives you away: it reads like a formula slogan. The short variant, "This isn't X. It's Y.", is just as recognizable.
    - Bad: "It's not just a planner. It's a transformation tool."
    - Write instead: "It's a planner for mapping out your week without losing track."

27. **Forced contrast**
    - Why it gives you away: it sets up a false opposition to sound smart.
    - Bad: "The strategy prioritizes human connection over cold metrics."
    - Write instead: "The strategy tracks how customers respond and adjusts the messages."

28. **Rule of three**
    - Why it gives you away: it uses triplets to sound complete.
    - Bad: "Simple, fast and efficient."
    - Write instead: "It takes under ten minutes."

29. **Elegant variation**
    - Why it gives you away: it avoids repeating normal words and ends up sounding like a thesaurus.
    - Bad: "The school serves children; the institution also welcomes teens; the facility…"
    - Write instead: "The school serves children and teens."

30. **Stacked abstract nouns**
    - Why it gives you away: it buries the action under "-tion", "-ment" and "-ity" nouns.
    - Bad: "The implementation of the improvement resulted in the optimization of communication."
    - Write instead: "After the change, the team answers faster."

31. **Soft universal statement**
    - Why it gives you away: it says something nobody would dispute and nobody needed to read.
    - Bad: "Communication is key to any successful business."
    - Write instead: "When nobody confirms the order, the customer emails three times."

32. **"It's important to note / worth noting"**
    - Why it gives you away: it sounds like a lecture and stalls the sentence. Same for "It's worth mentioning", "Keep in mind that", "It should be noted".
    - Bad: "It's important to note that results may vary."
    - Write instead: "Results ranged from 12% to 18% in our tests."

33. **"Whether you're X or Y"**
    - Why it gives you away: it's the default AI way to widen the audience, and it almost always opens a paragraph.
    - Bad: "Whether you're a seasoned pro or just starting out, this guide has something for you."
    - Write instead: "This guide is for people who've sold before but never priced a service."

34. **Chained transitions**
    - Why it gives you away: every paragraph opens with "Additionally,", "Moreover,", "Furthermore,", "That said,", "In addition,".
    - Bad: "Additionally, the tool saves time. Moreover, it improves management. Furthermore, it reduces errors."
    - Write instead: "It saves time because every open task sits in one place."

35. **Empty intensifiers**
    - Why it gives you away: "truly", "incredibly", "absolutely", "undoubtedly", "genuinely" prop up sentences that have no proof.
    - Bad: "This is undoubtedly the best option on the market."
    - Write instead: "It's the only one on the market that connects to your invoicing."

36. **Defensive "concrete"**
    - Why it gives you away: it shows up constantly in chatbot answers about criticism, proof and review ("concrete evidence", "concrete examples").
    - Bad: "Without concrete evidence, this criticism lacks merit."
    - Write instead: "Send me the part that bugged you and I'll take another look."

37. **Excessive hedging**
    - Why it gives you away: it protects itself in every part of the sentence ("arguably", "potentially", "in some contexts", "it could be said").
    - Bad: "It could arguably be considered a potentially useful option in certain contexts."
    - Write instead: "It works best for small teams."

38. **False certainty**
    - Why it gives you away: it promises a broad result without proof.
    - Bad: "This technique guarantees more sales."
    - Write instead: "This technique tends to lift response once the offer is already proven."

### Grammar and register

39. **No contractions**
    - Why it gives you away: casual English uses contractions everywhere. Copy full of "you are", "it is", "do not" sounds like a form letter or a machine.
    - Bad: "You will love it. It is easy, and you do not need any experience."
    - Write instead: "You'll pick it up fast. No experience needed."

40. **"From X to Y" false range**
    - Why it gives you away: it lists two things that aren't ends of any real scale just to sound sweeping.
    - Bad: "From startups to Fortune 500s, from marketing to mindset, we cover it all."
    - Write instead: "Most of our clients are agencies with 5 to 20 people."

41. **Passive voice by default**
    - Why it gives you away: it hides who did what. Fine now and then, a tell when it runs through the whole text.
    - Bad: "The report was prepared by the team and was sent to the partners."
    - Write instead: "The team wrote the report and sent it to the partners."

42. **Rhetorical question opener**
    - Why it gives you away: "Ever wondered…?", "What if I told you…?", "Imagine a world where…" is how a model starts copy when it has no real hook.
    - Bad: "Ever wondered why some brands just seem to click with their audience?"
    - Write instead: "Your last three posts got more saves than likes. That's a signal."

43. **Mixed spelling or register**
    - Why it gives you away: it mixes US and UK spelling, or jumps between "you" and "one", "we" and "the company". A person rarely does that. A model does it all the time.
    - Bad: "Organise your colors and optimize your behaviour. One should start today."
    - Write instead: "Organize your colors and fix your habits. Start today." (US) or "Organise your colours and sort out your habits. Start today." (UK)

### Formatting and typography

44. **Title Case where the format doesn't use it**
    - Why it gives you away: Title Case is correct for headlines in AP and Chicago style, but AI puts it on every heading, bullet, button and email subject. Most web copy, social posts and in-body headings use sentence case. Pick one per document and keep it.
    - Bad: "5 Ways To Build A Content Strategy That Actually Works" as a bullet inside an email.
    - Write instead: "5 ways to build a content strategy that works."

45. **Em dash overload**
    - Why it gives you away: it's the most talked-about AI tell in English. The model uses an em dash in nearly every sentence for dramatic pauses, often more than once.
    - Bad: "The method organizes your routine — no fuss — and boosts your results — fast."
    - Write instead: "The method organizes your routine without making it harder."

46. **Inconsistent dash style**
    - Why it gives you away: it mixes unspaced em dashes (US, Chicago), spaced en dashes (UK) and hyphens used as dashes in the same text.
    - Bad: "Sign up today—it's free - and start – right away."
    - Write instead: "Sign up today. It's free, and you can start right away."

47. **Catalog exclamations**
    - Why it gives you away: copy built from "Discover…!", "Don't miss out!", "Act now!" back to back.
    - Bad: "Discover the secret! Transform your life! Sign up now!"
    - Write instead: "Enrollment closes Friday. The link's in the bio."

48. **Too much bold**
    - Why it gives you away: it looks like an automatic attempt to make everything skimmable.
    - Bad: "You need **clarity**, **focus**, **execution** and **results**."
    - Write instead: "You need to know what you're selling, to whom and why."

49. **List with mini-headers**
    - Why it gives you away: it turns a paragraph into "Problem: / Solution: / Result:".
    - Bad: "Diagnosis: initial review. Execution: plan applied. Outcome: growth."
    - Write instead: "First we looked at the data. Then we changed the plan."

50. **Unneeded vertical list**
    - Why it gives you away: it formats simple prose as a checklist to look organized.
    - Bad: "Benefit 1: speed. Benefit 2: control. Benefit 3: clarity."
    - Write instead: "You can see what's late without opening five spreadsheets."

51. **Emoji as structure**
    - Why it gives you away: it uses emoji to decorate headings or bullets instead of writing better.
    - Bad: "🚀 Results / ✅ Benefits / 🔥 Offer"
    - Write instead: "Results / Benefits / Offer"

52. **Unneeded table**
    - Why it gives you away: it forces structure where a sentence would be clearer.
    - Bad: "Metric | Value / Team | 12 people"
    - Write instead: "The team has 12 people."

53. **Inconsistent quotes and apostrophes**
    - Why it gives you away: it mixes curly and straight quotes and apostrophes from different tools.
    - Bad: "The client said “yes" and then 'maybe’."
    - Write instead: "The client said 'yes' and then 'maybe.'"

54. **Inconsistent numbers, dates and serial commas**
    - Why it gives you away: it writes "10" in one line and "ten" in the next, mixes 03/04 and 4 March, or uses the Oxford comma only some of the time.
    - Bad: "Join 3 sessions on 03/04, 10 April and May fifth with Ana, Leo and Rui, and Mia."
    - Write instead: "Join three sessions on March 4, April 10 and May 5." (and pick one comma style for the whole text)

55. **Markdown leftovers**
    - Why it gives you away: it exposes the draft layer.
    - Bad: "## **Unmissable** offer [click here](url)"
    - Write instead: format for the real channel or write plain text.

56. **Placeholder text**
    - Why it gives you away: it shows a template got pasted and never finished.
    - Bad: "Hi [Name], here's the link: [insert link]."
    - Write instead: "Hi Marina, here's the link to the proposal."

57. **Divider before every heading**
    - Why it gives you away: a Markdown habit left over from a generated draft.
    - Bad: "---\nAbout the offer"
    - Write instead: "About the offer"

### Chatbot tone and behind the scenes

58. **Customer-service preamble**
    - Why it gives you away: it answers like an assistant instead of delivering the piece. "Certainly!", "Absolutely!", "Great question!", "Sure! Here's…".
    - Bad: "Certainly! Here's a more human-sounding version of your text:"
    - Write instead: deliver the version directly.

59. **Canned collaborative phrases**
    - Why it gives you away: "I hope this helps!", "Let me know if you'd like me to…", "Feel free to reach out" sound like an interface.
    - Bad: "I hope this helps! Let me know if you'd like any tweaks."
    - Write instead: offer a next step only when it's useful and specific.

60. **Instructions pasted into the final text**
    - Why it gives you away: the text talks to the user, not to the end reader.
    - Bad: "You can add this section to your sales page."
    - Write instead: write the section itself.

61. **Knowledge-cutoff disclaimer**
    - Why it gives you away: it exposes a model limitation instead of solving the writing.
    - Bad: "As of my last update, there is no public information about this."
    - Write instead: "I couldn't find public information on this in the sources provided."

62. **Speculating about missing sources**
    - Why it gives you away: it invents a reason for a gap.
    - Bad: "Details are scarce because she keeps a low profile."
    - Write instead: "The sources provided don't include details about her personal life."

63. **AI refusal or capability inside the text**
    - Why it gives you away: it leaves the chatbot conversation inside the artifact.
    - Bad: "As an AI, I can't confirm that."
    - Write instead: "There isn't enough evidence to say that."

64. **Abrupt cut-off**
    - Why it gives you away: the generation stopped mid-thought.
    - Bad: "The company has grown in recent years and since then…"
    - Write instead: finish the sentence or cut the passage.

65. **Commenting on the version's quality**
    - Why it gives you away: it says the text got better instead of simply being better.
    - Bad: "This version is more engaging, persuasive and clear."
    - Write instead: deliver the text without the self-review.

### Claims, sources and data

66. **Fabricated precision**
    - Why it gives you away: invented percentages, user counts or "studies show" figures give false authority.
    - Bad: "87% of entrepreneurs use the method to organize their routine."
    - Write instead: "Many of our clients use the method to organize their week." Only include numbers that were provided.

67. **Broken or suspicious link**
    - Why it gives you away: AI often invents URLs that look real.
    - Bad: "Source: examplemagazine.com/study-2025-final"
    - Write instead: use a verified source or say no source was provided.

68. **Invalid or unverifiable citation**
    - Why it gives you away: a fake DOI, ISBN, study title or page creates false authority.
    - Bad: "According to Smith (2024), p. 999…"
    - Write instead: cite only details the user gave or that were verified.

69. **Book cited without a page**
    - Why it gives you away: it looks academic but doesn't let anyone check the claim.
    - Bad: "According to the book Modern Marketing, this increases conversion."
    - Write instead: "According to Modern Marketing, p. 42…" or remove the claim.

70. **Chatbot link residue**
    - Why it gives you away: `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `utm_source=openai` or similar reveal an automated origin.
    - Bad: "site.com/article?utm_source=chatgpt.com"
    - Write instead: "site.com/article"

71. **Citation artifact**
    - Why it gives you away: leftover codes like `turn0search0`, `oaicite`, `contentReference` or `[cite: 1]`.
    - Bad: "The school was founded in 1990 `turn0search0`."
    - Write instead: "The school was founded in 1990, according to its website."

### Context and consistency

72. **Sudden voice shift**
    - Why it gives you away: a friendly brand suddenly sounds like a legal notice.
    - Bad: "Please be advised that operational enhancements have been implemented."
    - Write instead: "We changed how we handle requests so we can get back to you faster."

73. **Wrong variant or register**
    - Why it gives you away: it uses a variety of English that doesn't match the audience, or mixes vocabulary from different countries.
    - Bad: "Grab your trainers and meet us at the parking lot." (UK and US in one sentence)
    - Write instead: "Grab your sneakers and meet us at the parking lot." (US) or "Grab your trainers and meet us at the car park." (UK)

74. **Over-exhaustive summary**
    - Why it gives you away: small changes get a huge formal justification.
    - Bad: "I've refined the text to ensure clarity, neutrality, flow and alignment with best practices."
    - Write instead: "I fixed the tone and cut the repetition."

75. **Note to the evaluator**
    - Why it gives you away: it tries to convince the reviewer instead of improving the text.
    - Bad: "Note to reviewer: this text is neutral and well-sourced."
    - Write instead: remove the note. Let the text and the sources do the work.

76. **Process marker in the final text**
    - Why it gives you away: it leaves the workflow inside the artifact.
    - Bad: "[Review later] [Add source] [Approved]"
    - Write instead: resolve the marker or delete it.

77. **Model idiolect**
    - Why it gives you away: the text has a recognizable model flavor: broad context, polished positivity, pseudo-scientific words.
    - Bad: "The decision empirically correlates with causal engagement dynamics."
    - Write instead: "The decision seems tied to the jump in comments and replies."

## What not to treat as proof

Don't assume a text was AI-generated just because it has good grammar, formal language, a technical tone, no citations, correct formatting, a letter structure, a single em dash or one transition word. The problem is the combination: generic structure, inflated promises, vague authority, repeated vocabulary, faux-casual hooks, chatbot preambles and formatting leftovers.

## Final checklist

Before delivering any text for a person to read, confirm:

- The first line starts with a concrete situation, tension, image or promise, not "In today's fast-paced world…", "Ever wondered…?" or "Imagine…".
- Every claim comes from facts the user gave or is clearly framed as opinion or a position.
- No inflated words are left: delve, tapestry, testament, landscape, realm, pivotal, crucial, robust, seamless, vibrant, leverage, harness, foster, elevate, unlock, empower, game-changer, cutting-edge, all-in-one solution.
- No template phrases are left: "plays a pivotal role", "not only… but also", "it's not just", "it's important to note", "whether you're", "here's the thing", "let's dive in", "in conclusion", "I hope this helps".
- No trailing "-ing" analysis clauses (highlighting, underscoring, showcasing) and no "serves as" where "is" works.
- No vague authority is left: experts, critics, studies, research, the market, the media, people say.
- Sentences use plain verbs where possible: is, has, uses, does, sells, shows, cuts, fixes.
- Contractions sound like the channel, and the English variant (US, UK…) is the same from start to finish.
- Em dashes are rare and in one style; capitalization, quotes, numbers, dates and serial commas each follow one rule.
- The formatting fits the channel and has no Markdown leftovers, placeholders, citation artifacts or decorative emoji.
- The rhythm sounds like one specific person, not a model filling in blanks.
- The CTA, if there is one, sounds like a human invitation or instruction, not a billboard.
- The final answer contains the ready-to-use text, not commentary on how it was improved, unless the user asks for it.
